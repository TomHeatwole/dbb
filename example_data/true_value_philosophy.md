# What should "True Hwang Value" actually mean?

*A philosophical companion to the Hwang True Simulator results. No numbers are
derived here — this is the reasoning framework for deciding how to apply the
multipliers we measured.*

---

## The tension in one sentence

The simulator measures how much a player's **current-season production** is
worth in the Hwang format relative to his price — but a dynasty asset is a
claim on **many seasons** of production, and it is not obvious whether the
correction we measured should apply to one season or to all of them.

Your instinct ("if future scoring is also going to be 1.3× higher for an RB,
you could almost argue all its future value deserves the multiplier") is
half right, and the half that's right matters a lot. The key is that the
multiplier we measure is not one thing. It is a braid of three strands, and
each strand behaves differently over time.

---

## The three strands inside a multiplier

Take the comp-basis Hwang RB multiplier (~1.3×). Unbraided:

**Strand 1 — the format effect.**
3 RB slots + 2 FLEX + 0 PPR structurally raises what RB production is worth
*in this league*, every week, forever, as long as the rules don't change.
This is the Hwang ÷ Regular "format factor" (~1.15–1.25 for RB). It is a
property of the *rules*, not of the market or the player.

**Strand 2 — market mispricing of the current season.**
Even in a vanilla format, the market (KTC/ADP) systematically misprices some
current-season production — e.g. it overpays for WR receptions that don't
exist in standard scoring, or underrates grinder RBs. This strand is a
property of the *market's redraft judgment*, and it may or may not persist
year to year.

**Strand 3 — the longevity discount (in the KTC basis only).**
KTC dynasty prices already discount RBs for short careers and age cliffs.
When we pair players by *dynasty* price and compare one season of production,
RBs look cheap — but part of that cheapness is *correct compensation* for the
seasons they won't have. This strand is not mispricing at all. It's the
market being right about the future while we measure only the present.

The comp basis mostly removes strand 3 (comp values are already
current-season-denominated), which is why the comp run is the cleaner
measurement of strands 1+2.

---

## Position A: "Multiply everything" (full-multiplier dynasty value)

**True Value = Dynasty price × m(v)**

The argument: the league is best-ball, the rules are stable, and every future
season will also be played under Hwang rules. If RB production is worth 1.3×
per market dollar this year, it will be again next year. So the whole asset
deserves the multiplier.

**Where it's right:** for strand 1. The format effect genuinely applies to
every future season. A 2029 RB point is worth just as much extra as a 2026
RB point.

**Where it breaks:** for strand 3. The RB longevity discount in the dynasty
price is the market *correctly* telling you that the RB has fewer future
seasons. If you multiply the whole dynasty price by a factor that was
partly generated by that discount (the KTC-basis multiplier), you are paying
yourself twice for the same fact: once when the market discounted the price,
and again when you inflated your valuation because the discounted price made
the current season look cheap. The full KTC-basis multiplier applied to full
dynasty value systematically over-credits short-career positions.

**Verdict:** philosophically coherent only if you use the *format factor*
(strand 1 alone), not the full measured multiplier.

---

## Position B: "Current season only" (the purist HVORP view)

**True Value = future component at market price + current season × m(v)**

The argument: our evidence is one-season evidence. We plugged players into
rosters and measured one season of best-ball points. We have measured
*nothing* about future seasons, so intellectual honesty says: correct only
the part you measured, and take the market's word for the rest.

**Where it's right:** it never overclaims. It's the minimal correction
consistent with the data.

**Where it breaks:** it quietly assumes the format effect vanishes after
this season. But the format effect is the *most* durable strand of the
three — it is literally written into the league constitution. Refusing to
apply it forward means you will systematically underpay for RBs in trades
every single year, and some other owner who groks the format will happily
farm that edge against you annually.

**Verdict:** too conservative. It treats the strongest part of the signal as
if it were the weakest.

---

## Position C: The decomposition (apply each strand to what it governs)

This is where the reasoning naturally lands:

```
True Hwang Value =
    current-season component × full comp-basis multiplier m_comp(v)
  + future component        × format factor f(v)        (Hwang ÷ Regular)
```

- The **current season** gets the full correction — format effect plus
  market redraft error — because that's exactly what the comp-basis
  simulation measured.
- The **future component** gets only the format factor, because:
  - the format effect provably persists (the rules persist), while
  - the market's aging/longevity pricing embedded in the future component is
    the best available estimate of future *quantities*, and we have no
    evidence it's wrong — only that this year's production is worth more
    per point in Hwang.

A practical note on the split: your redraft value index already implies a
current-vs-future decomposition (competitor value ≈ the current-season
component of a dynasty price; dynasty price minus that ≈ future component).
The pieces exist in the pipeline today.

---

## Two deeper wrinkles worth having an opinion on

**1. Does the *mispricing* strand persist too?**
There's a real argument that strand 2 also recurs: markets have overrated
PPR-flavored WRs every year for a decade, and there's no reason ADP suddenly
gets wise. If you believe the market will keep making the same redraft error
annually, then even the future component deserves a bit more than the pure
format factor — because when you eventually *sell* that future, you'll be
selling to the same biased market... which cuts the other way! Persistent
mispricing helps you on the buy side (keep buying underpriced RB seasons) but
does **not** raise the hold value of the asset, because market bias moves
trade prices, not points scored. The clean way to say it: **mispricing is a
strategy edge, not an asset attribute.** You harvest it transactionally,
every year, by trading; you don't capitalize it into a player's sticker
value. That's the strongest argument for keeping the future component at
format-factor-only.

**2. Value to whom? Consumption vs. exchange.**
A win-now team *consumes* production: for them, points are the terminal good
and the corrected values are "true." A rebuilder mostly *exchanges* assets:
for them, a player is worth what the (uncorrected, biased) market will pay,
and applying Hwang corrections to a player they'll flip in 8 months is
valuing him in a currency they'll never spend. So "True Hwang Value" is
genuinely different for the two postures — which is philosophically tidy,
because you already maintain separate competitor and rebuilder indexes. The
corrections belong on the competitor index; the rebuilder index should stay
closer to market.

---

## Where that leaves the choice

| | current season | future seasons |
|---|---|---|
| **A. multiply everything** | full m(v) | full m(v) — *double-counts longevity* |
| **B. current only** | full m(v) | 1.0 — *throws away the durable format edge* |
| **C. decomposition** | full m(v) | format factor f(v) |

Position C is the one that survives scrutiny: every strand of the measured
multiplier gets applied exactly to the horizon it's evidence about. Your
"future scoring is also 1.3× higher" intuition is vindicated for the format
strand — future RB points really are worth more here, forever — but the
longevity discount inside dynasty prices was never a mispricing, so it must
not be re-credited.

And one honest caveat to end on: the difference between A, B, and C is
largest exactly where careers are shortest (RB). For QBs and young WRs the
three positions of the dial give similar answers. So the philosophical
choice is, in practice, mostly a referendum on how you want to price
veteran RBs — which is fitting, because that's the oldest argument in
dynasty fantasy football.
